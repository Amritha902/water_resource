Part 2 · The machine-learning extension

MAACC only reacts: every signal its agents see is a consequence of a disturbance that has already hit the plant. The influent, which is the main disturbance, is measured at the inlet and is largely predictable. This part adds models that predict, and tests each one on data it never saw, against a simple baseline.

1. Architecture

u(k, i) = b(k, i) + g(k, i) · a(k, i) + f(k, i) expert gated adaptive anticipatory prior (PID) critic term (MAACC) feed-forward (new)

Setting g ≡ 1 and f ≡ 0 gives back the paper's eq. (7) exactly, so PANDA is a strict generalisation: whatever stability argument holds for MAACC holds for that restriction. g ∈ (0, 1] and |f| ≤ f_max are explicitly bounded.

① Influent forecaster

Dilated causal TCN (dilations 1–32, width 64) over 24 h of inlet flow, NH₄ and readily biodegradable COD at 15-min resolution. It outputs 10/50/90 % quantiles for each of the next eight 15-min steps (2 h) and a dry/rain/storm classifier. Quantiles are kept in order by construction (cumulative softplus). Trained with the pinball loss on 160 random 14-day influents. src/wwtp/forecast/

② Neural digital twin

GRU (hidden 96) on a 6-min grid: h_t = GRU([s_t, u_t, d_t], h_{t−1}), s_{t+1} = s_t + g(h_t), plus an effluent-NH₄ head. Its recurrent state stands in for the unknown coupling G_i of eq. (4). It is differentiable in u, so the controller can ask “what if I move now?”. Trained on 60 % open-loop PRBS and 40 % dithered-PID data, with scheduled sampling. src/wwtp/twin/

③ Anticipatory feed-forward f

Every 6 min, the twin is rolled 48 min ahead with a differentiable copy of the PID in the loop, twice: once under the forecast influent and once with the influent frozen. Only the difference between the two rollouts is penalised, so any standing twin bias cancels and f = 0 whenever the forecast predicts no change. f is found by 6 Adam steps on that objective, with a one-sided penalty on a predicted rise in effluent NH₄. panda/controller.py

④ Context + uncertainty gate g

The agents' networks also see a 6-number forecast summary (predicted relative change of flow at 1 h and 2 h, of NH₄ and COD at 2 h, flow uncertainty, P(wet)). The learned term is scaled by g = 1 / (1 + 4·(q90 − q10)/q50), so when the forecast is unsure the controller falls back to the PID prior.

Weather

The regime classifier does not generalise

The previously reported 95.3 % dry/rain/storm accuracy came from a validation split drawn at random from overlapping windows of the same scenarios, which leaks. On fresh scenarios it is near chance (below). The label is also ill-posed: each window carries its scenario's weather, but on the calm days of a “storm” scenario nothing in 24 h of history can show a storm is coming. The fix is a per-window label (“is a wet-weather event in this window?”). Until then the P(wet) signal PANDA receives is close to noise.

3. Neural digital twin

Two checks, both on data the twin never saw. Step tests: the real plant and the twin, settled under constant dry-average influent, receive the same actuator step. Free-running prediction: from a fresh rollout, the twin is warmed on 1.6 h of history and then predicts 4.8 h ahead on its own outputs, given the real actuator moves and influent.

Closed-loop identification bias, and why the PRBS data is not optional. The first twin was trained only on PID-controlled data. With the PID in the loop, KLa₅ rises exactly when oxygen demand rises, so in that data more air does not visibly mean more oxygen. The twin learned a near-zero aeration gain (40 → 220 1/d moved DO by 0.05 g/m³), and differentiating it moved the actuator the wrong way. Refitting with 60 % open-loop pseudo-random excitation fixed the signs, as the step tests above show. tests/test_learned_models.py::test_trained_twin_has_the_right_control_gains

4. PANDA in closed loop

Two operating conditions. Paper protocol: instantaneous actuators, 3 % actuator noise and sensor noise. Realistic instrumentation: the same noise, plus blower and pump slew limits (6 1/d and 2,200 m³/d per 45 s, so full travel takes about 30 min), a 2-min DO-probe lag and a nitrate analyser that updates every 10 min. Feedback is slow in the realistic setting, which is where a forecast should help most.

5. Effluent-ammonium early warning

The question, every 6 minutes: will effluent NH₄-N break the 4 g/m³ discharge limit within 12, 30, 60, 90 or 120 min, and how high will it go? Inputs: only signals a plant already measures online, namely inlet flow, NH₄ and COD; DO₅ and NO₂ probes; the controller's own KLa₅ and Qₐ; the effluent NH₄ analyser (noisy, and stale between 12-min cycles); water temperature; time of day; and the forecaster's 6-number outlook. Why the plants vary: at the paper's settings effluent NH₄ never exceeds 2.4 g/m³, so there is nothing to warn about. Real plants trim aeration to save energy and lose nitrification in cold water. Each simulated plant therefore draws aeration in reactors 3–4 from 160–240 1/d, temperature from 11.5–17 °C (BSM2 Arrhenius factors on the nitrifier growth and decay rates) and the DO set point from 0.8–2.0. Train, validation and test plants are disjoint: 96, 16 and 24 plants of 10 days each.

Model

Causal TCN (dilations 1–16, width 64) over 6 h of the 17 signals. Level head: 10/50/90 % quantiles of effluent NH₄ at each horizon. Hazard head: a discrete-time survival model. For each horizon interval it predicts the probability λ_j that the first exceedance happens there, and P(violation within h_j) = 1 − ∏(1 − λ_l). The warning probability therefore cannot decrease as the horizon grows. src/wwtp/warning/

How it is scored

Against today's practice (alarm when the analyser reads above the limit), trend extrapolation of the analyser, and gradient boosting on hand-made features of the same signals. The main scores use only moments when the plant is still compliant, because on an ongoing violation copying the analyser is trivially right. Operationally, each method's alarm threshold is set on validation plants to allow at most 1 false alarm per compliant day, and lead time is measured on test plants.
